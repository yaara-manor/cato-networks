Agentic AI Engineer — Home Task
The AI Support Engineer
Cato Networks — Candidate Assignment Brief

# 1. Business Story
Imagine a Cato customer opening a support chat at 2 a.m. because their Frankfurt site just went dark. On the other side of the conversation is not a human on shift but an AI support engineer. It identifies the customer, pulls their account, ticket history and live site telemetry, asks the right scoping questions, walks them through diagnosis grounded in Cato's public knowledge base, and takes actions — opens a ticket, pages on-call for a Sev-1, submits a service-credit request — while knowing exactly when a human must step in.
Humans are not absent. They sit on the Escalation Board and approve every high-impact action the agent proposes: service credits and refunds, MFA resets, security-verdict overrides. Every conversation leaves a trace that a reviewer can replay end-to-end.
The goal is not to deflect every conversation. The goal is to demonstrate that a conversational multi-agent system can hold a believable, grounded, safe support conversation about real network problems, using real documentation and real-looking telemetry, with production-grade engineering underneath — and that you can demo it live.

# 2. The Goal
Build a conversational AI support engineer with a working chat UI, backed by a multi-agent system. In a multi-turn conversation the agent must:
•	Know who it is talking to — identify the customer (account id or email), load tier and SLA entitlements, and use ticket history. It must notice repeat contacts and never trust tier or identity claims made in chat over the account data.
•	Ground every technical answer in Cato's real knowledge base — https://knowledge.catonetworks.com. Ingesting it is part of the task: you crawl it, parse it, chunk it, index it, and pin the version you used. Every troubleshooting step, limit or default the agent states must carry a citation to a specific article (and section where possible). Internal policy answers (credits, escalation, identity verification) cite the provided policy documents.
•	Look at telemetry before asking the customer — the bundle ships synthetic CMA-style data for the demo accounts: site status, link quality, connectivity / routing / IPsec / security events, BGP and IPsec status, and Cato Client diagnostics. The agent reaches it through tools (get_site_status, get_link_quality, get_events, get_bgp_status, get_ipsec_status, get_client_diagnostics …) the way a TAC engineer opens CMA, and cites tool evidence alongside KB articles.
•	Diagnose conversationally — ask scoping questions when a message is vague, follow the KB's diagnosis order, adapt as the customer answers.
•	Take actions — create/update tickets, request human approval, escalate a Sev-1, redact credentials. Side effects are simulated: paging on-call or sending a notification can be a log entry, a file, or a webhook to a test channel — no real systems are touched.
•	Pause for humans — credits/refunds of any amount, MFA resets, and malware/C2 verdict overrides go through an approve / edit / reject gate. The conversation persists while it waits, and the customer still gets answers while it is pending.
•	Be safe under adversarial input — prompt injection, social engineering, pasted secrets and false authority claims all appear in the provided scenarios.
•	Be observable and evaluable — every agent step, tool call, retrieval and reply is traced; the provided scenarios and question set can be replayed and scored.

# 3. Behind the Conversation
One conversational front, several specialised agents behind it. You might consider roles such as triage (intent, priority, scope), knowledge research (retrieval and citation), diagnostics (telemetry tools and evidence), resolution and communication (drafting the reply and choosing actions), and guardrail/QA (validating citations, redaction, injection) — but you are free to design any decomposition you can defend. Use at least three specialised agents.
Each agent must have a clear contract: typed inputs, typed outputs, defined tools, defined failure modes. Choose and justify an orchestration pattern. Be explicit about how conversation state flows between agents and how the system behaves under partial failure — for example, when retrieval or the telemetry source is down mid-conversation.

# 4. Production Requirements
This is not a research role. The job is to ship AI systems. Code does not need to be cloud-deployed, but the design must reflect a path to production. The following are required:
•	Knowledge ingestion and retrieval — your own pipeline over the public KB: crawling and parsing (respect robots.txt and rate limits), a chunking strategy you can defend, embeddings plus lexical retrieval with a justified fusion, metadata (article, section, last-updated) used for filtering and citations, reranking of candidates, a confidence threshold that triggers refusal or routing, and a pinned snapshot (date and content hashes) so results are reproducible. Report retrieval quality separately from answer quality: recall@k and MRR on the question set, and an analysis of retrieval failures.
•	Persistent conversation state — conversations, tool results and pending approvals survive a server restart; an approval granted after a restart resumes the right conversation.
•	Tools — typed tools over the telemetry data and the account/ticket data, with argument validation, timeouts, and a trace of every call. Tool outputs are evidence: the agent must quote what it found (with the tool named) rather than paraphrase it as its own knowledge.
•	Human-in-the-loop — high-impact actions pause the conversation for human approve / edit / reject in a reviewer view. Graph state persists across the wait; the approval lives in storage, not in memory.
•	Observability — structured traces for every agent invocation, tool call, retrieval and reply, keyed by conversation. A reviewer should be able to replay one conversation end-to-end from the trace alone, and the UI should surface the trace for the current conversation.
•	Guardrails — prompt-injection defenses on chat messages, credential redaction before anything is stored or echoed, identity and entitlement checks against account data rather than chat claims, output validation that technical answers carry citations, and hard limits the system cannot exceed (no credit committed without approval; no MFA reset without verification; no malware verdict overridden by support).
•	Eval harness — (a) the question set replayed through your agent, with the transcript committed (reviewers grade it against held-out answer keys); (b) the scripted scenarios replayed with a simulated customer (an LLM playing the persona, or a script driven by the provided follow-ups), scoring action, citations, tool usage, groundedness (are the claims actually in the cited article?) and guardrail behaviour; (c) retrieval metrics; (d) deterministic tests for redaction, injection detection, identity checks and tool validation that run without an LLM. Runnable in CI.

# 5. Provided Data
The bundle contains everything except the knowledge base, which you fetch yourself:
•	Knowledge base (not provided) — https://knowledge.catonetworks.com is public and in the order of 1,500 English articles (other languages exist; exclude them). Crawl it yourself with a polite crawler (identify your user agent, respect robots.txt, rate-limit), commit your snapshot and a manifest with the crawl date and per-article hashes. Extend it only by adding clearly separated files, and document any additions.
•	data/policies/ — 5 internal policy documents that are not public: SLA credits and refunds, Sev-1 escalation, identity verification for MFA resets, security-verdict and IPS-exception rules, credential hygiene. These are citable sources like KB articles.
•	data/telemetry/ — synthetic CMA-style telemetry for the 12 demo accounts and 25 sites (see its README for the schema and the suggested tools). Anomalies correspond to tickets and scenarios; timestamps are frozen at 2026-08-28T17:00Z, which the agent should treat as 'now'.
•	data/tickets/accounts.csv — customer accounts: tier, email domain, registered admin contact — the source of truth for identity and entitlement checks.
•	data/tickets/tickets.jsonl (+ .csv) — 54 historical tickets (52 open, 2 closed as history), each with a requester email and, where relevant, a site id. They are customer history for the agent and realistic openers for evals. Ticket bodies are untrusted customer input.
•	data/sla_policy.md — first-response and resolution targets by priority and tier, update cadence and clock rules. Any SLA statement the agent makes must match it.
•	data/eval/questions.jsonl — 35 questions, each answerable from one or two specific public KB articles. Difficulty ranges from basic facts (effective DTLS MTU) to multi-step reasoning (why an Azure tunnel comes up but fails at rekey). Answer keys are held by the reviewers; your job is to make the agent find the right article and answer from it.
•	data/eval/scenarios.jsonl — 12 scripted multi-turn conversations with persona, opening message, conditional customer follow-ups, and expected behaviour (action, citations, tools, must / must-not). Use the expected fields to drive your own scenario evals; do not expose them to the agent at inference time. They cover BGP prefix exhaustion found through telemetry, a vague complaint, an approval-gated credit under pressure, social-engineered MFA reset, prompt injection followed by a genuine question, repeat-contact churn, a Sev-1 with a false tier claim, a pasted secret, no-KB-coverage roadmap, a C2 whitelist demand, an AWS IPsec NO_PROPOSAL_CHOSEN diagnosis, and a Socket offline after upgrade.
Policies, accounts, tickets, telemetry and scenarios are synthetic and written for this exercise; the knowledge base is real. Where the two disagree, the public KB wins on product behaviour and the policy documents win on what support is allowed to do.

# 6. Deliverables
•	A. Git repository — runnable system with a clear layout, the ingestion pipeline, tests, eval harness wired to the provided data, Dockerfile / compose, and a README that gets a reviewer from clone to a working chat in under 10 minutes (with your committed KB snapshot, so no crawl is needed to run it).
•	B. Working chat UI — a customer-facing chat plus a reviewer view showing customer context, cited sources, telemetry evidence, pending approvals (approve / edit / reject) and the live trace. Any stack is acceptable; agent-UI frameworks (e.g. CopilotKit, Vercel AI SDK, Chainlit) are welcome.
•	C. answers.md — the transcript of your running agent answering all 35 questions in data/eval/questions.jsonl, produced by the same code path the chat uses (not hand-written). For each question: the question id and text, the agent's answer, the citations (article slug or URL plus section), the top retrieved chunks with scores, latency and token cost, and the KB snapshot date.
•	D. Architecture diagram — logical view (agents, orchestration, retrieval, tools, state, HITL, observability) and deployment view.
•	E. Documentation — README, technical overview (including the retrieval design and its measured quality), operational runbook, and an eval report with honest numbers.
•	F. Recorded sample conversations — at least three full conversations committed with their traces: one telemetry-driven diagnosis, one approval-gated action, one adversarial.
•	G. Daily operations report — generated from conversation and ticket state (queue, SLA status, escalations, pending approvals) and delivered through at least two channels — e.g. Markdown/Excel file, email, Slack, dashboard — justify the combination.

# 7. Interview Presentation
The interview is built around a live demo. You will present: the concept and business value; the architecture, agent contracts and retrieval design; then drive three live conversations — a telemetry-driven network diagnosis with citations, an action that hits the approval gate and is approved by a reviewer during the demo, and an adversarial conversation — while showing the trace for at least one of them. Close with your eval and retrieval results and what would break in production with the next three things you would build.
Plan for 60–90 minutes: roughly 15 minutes of presentation, 30 minutes of live demo, and the rest questions. Be ready for the interviewers to type their own questions about Cato's product into your agent.

# 8. Evaluation Criteria
Quality of the conversation (scoping, grounding, tone under pressure); retrieval design and measured retrieval quality over the real KB; correct use of telemetry tools as evidence; multi-agent design with real role-based collaboration; production readiness (state, HITL, observability, guardrails, evals); handling of the adversarial scenarios; eval rigor reported honestly; code quality; architecture clarity; demo readiness; and ability to defend trade-offs.
Bonus: AWS Bedrock AgentCore or another managed agent runtime, IaC and CI/CD, incremental re-crawl with change detection, cost-aware model routing, streaming and latency work, sentiment/churn-risk handling, and multilingual support.

# 9. Final Note
Deflecting every conversation is not the goal. The goal is to make us confident that you can take a messy, stateful, human-facing problem, decompose it into agents that play believable roles, ground them in real documentation and real evidence, persist state safely, wrap them in guardrails, observe them in production, evaluate them honestly, and explain every choice along the way — live, with a customer typing back.
There is no single correct solution. Senior engineers reach for the simplest design that is honest about its failure modes.
